Lately, I've been thinking about where I want to go next.

In this era, AI has become astonishingly capable. From producing solutions to Millennium Prize–level math problems, to cracking century-old ciphers, to making discoveries in biology, AI seems able to do almost anything. So what is left for us to do beyond AI?

I think the future will need people in two kinds of places. First, wherever correctness is hard to verify in the short term, AI is hard to replace. Without a reliable feedback signal, AI can neither iterate on itself nor be trained to improve—and the results AI produces most easily today are the ones that can be judged quickly against a fixed standard. Second, using agents well and producing efficiently is itself a kind of value that individuals can create on top of AI.

From this, we can work out how to prepare for the future. For directions where there is no fixed standard for judging whether the final goal has been achieved, we need a bottom-to-top understanding of the whole system—including learning some things AI already knows. On the other hand, if a direction's ultimate goal already has a fixed standard of correctness, and it does not serve as the foundation for some field that AI can hardly replace, then people probably don't need to learn that kind of knowledge.

To find a direction that suits me, I need some judgment and a sense of where the current frontier lies. I asked AI to tell me about leading figures in various fields, then gathered information about them online. I followed news in AI safety, high-performance networking, embodied intelligence, quantum computing, BCI, and AI for AI infra, and eventually found that I'm most interested in high-performance networking and AI for AI infra. On one hand, I have prior experience in cluster operations and high-performance computing competitions; on the other, I think these areas are in real demand, not easily replaced by AI, and have a high barrier to entry with relatively few competitors. For high-performance networking on clusters, many problems only surface once you scale up in practice, and this slow feedback calls for people with their own ideas to steer the overall direction—something AI can't directly replace—while also requiring a deep foundation in networking. For AI for AI infra, you have to prevent agents from reward hacking, while also accounting for long-term maintainability and making sure optimizations are genuinely insightful. None of this can be judged by a single simple metric, so AI can't just iterate on it by itself. At the same time, it's an opportunity to use agents well to optimize infrastructure, which is a direction I find very exciting.

But what if AI becomes so capable that it can replace all of this too? If by then AI capability is monopolized by a handful of leading companies, that is indeed something that makes me pessimistic. Still, I think making some achievements during this window and gaining some resources to lay the groundwork for what comes next is worthwhile.

Whatever happens, actively exploring, proactively connecting with resources, talking with people, and cutting losses early when a direction isn't right are always the right moves. More importantly, the value of the future lies not only in what we achieve, but in our curiosity about the world, and in always keeping our passion and sense of anticipation.
